# Todora

A 3rd-person racer in [Rust](https://www.rust-lang.org/) and [Bevy](https://bevy.org/). Drive the Omarchy GT #95 around forty scaled circuits, with **T** for the list of them.

The circuits are laid out from public GPS traces of real circuits, and each carries a name of its own that nods to where it is — Parco Reale, Ardennes Loop, Figure-Eight Hills, Styrian Bowl. Todora is not affiliated with any circuit, series or team, and uses none of their names or marks. Internally a circuit keeps the id it has always had (`monza`, `spa-francorchamps`), which boards, saved laps and the weekly challenge are keyed to, so renaming one never touches a time; the circuit search finds a circuit by either.

The default garage choice is the **OMARCHY GT #95**, an invented endurance GT built in Blender, in the pale blue and orange DHH raced in: twin hexagonal intakes with yellow endurance lamps, a full-width tail light, animated race wheels, rear wing and diffuser, **OMARCHY RACING** on the wing and **DHH** on the plate. It is modelled on no real car and carries no real makers or sponsors. Clubman and Express remain handling and colour variants of the shared model. [Model and rebuild instructions](docs/models/omarchy-gt-95.md).

The GT measures about **1.06 game units long and 0.45 across the body**, with matching wheel contacts and ghost models. Every road is **3.6 m wide** including kerbs (previously 3.3 m), with 2.94 m of asphalt. Its aerodynamic grip contribution is twice the earlier estate car's, with the garage variants retaining their different setups.

Acceleration builds more gradually: the default GT on flat road reaches displayed 150 km/h in about 2.9 seconds and 200 in 5.0 seconds. It approaches 95% of its roughly 230 km/h top speed after 6.8 seconds.

**Sound:** [CLIamp's Omarchy station](https://radio.cliamp.stream/omarchy/stream.pls) plays quietly in the background. **N** switches music on or off; **F8** switches tyre sound on or off. The two are independent — the radio can play with the wheels quiet, and the other way around. Both can also be toggled from the pause menu. An engine note, synthesised on the audio thread from a few harmonics and a little filtered noise, climbs through six gears with speed and hardens under throttle; it follows the tyre toggle and has its own volume in **Settings → Audio**. `cargo test --locked --lib write_the_engine -- --ignored` renders twelve seconds of it to `dist/engine.wav`. Recorded wheel contact stays audible whenever the car moves, including coasting, with a deep, muffled rolling rumble that grows with speed. Rubber squeal builds near the grip limit and becomes brighter during a slide. Wheels go quiet at rest and on pause; grass keeps the rolling texture without tarmac squeal. Music continues through menus. [Tyre sample credits and license](assets/audio/CREDITS.md). All sound fades out when the window loses focus. Radio needs an internet connection and reconnects automatically; tyre feedback works offline. Music is streamed live, not bundled with the app.

All forty laps are shorter: most by about 10%, with smaller reductions on the tightest circuits to keep the roads separate. Finished laps range from 446 m at Magny-Cours to 1,807 m at Baku. The shared plan scale is 0.12 and the elevation scale is 0.252; corner opening targets 4.5 m. The menu lists the rebuilt lengths. Surface fingerprints automatically reject ghosts recorded on the previous layouts.

**Pause controls:** arrows, D-pad or left stick select a highlighted action; **Enter / A** activates it. Held directions repeat at the same pace. **Esc / B / Start** resumes, and reopening selects Resume.

**Ghost reset:** press **Esc**, then **Reset this ghost** (current circuit and driving mode) or **Reset all ghosts**. Activate the same button again to confirm; moving selection cancels confirmation. This removes the corresponding saved best times and restarts your lap. **Cmd+Q** on macOS, **Ctrl+Q** elsewhere, or **Quit game** in the pause menu exits with audio stopped and radio shutdown signalled; native window/menu quit also cleans up sound.

Each circuit sits on a grass rectangle extending 50 metres beyond the outermost verge. The verge and fill meet at road height and share a [natural grass texture](assets/textures/README.md); the raised kerbs remain 5 cm proud. Suzuka’s bridge has an obsidian deck, slim graphite rails, three underside girders and retaining walls fitted to the rendered terrain. Closed approach ends prevent gaps beneath the shoulders; the lower road remains open through the span. Circuit surfaces use unlit materials and shadow maps are disabled, so steep hills and the underpass stay readable. Grass and asphalt use world-space tiling, mipmaps and anisotropic filtering, with painted lines and kerbs kept crisp. The changed verge profile gives tracks new fingerprints, so previously saved ghosts are rejected rather than replayed against different ground.

Fair-weather clouds drift slowly across the sky and melt into the horizon haze. The sky is one opaque, unlit dome that follows the camera, painted once at startup on a worker thread. Each frame costs one draw call, one texture read per pixel of sky and one transform update. There is no transparency, per-pixel noise or texture upload during play; until the painting is ready (a fraction of a second), the previous plain sky shows.

The chase camera keeps its level boom on flat and uphill road. On steep descents, or when a crest or bank rises between camera and car, it lifts just enough to keep the car in sight, including when speed stretches the boom. It reads the same road and terrain heights the wheels use, costing under 10 µs a frame, with no mesh raycasts. Follow smoothing, the cut on reset and scroll zoom are unchanged.

Braking boards reading **150**, **100** and **50** stand on the outside verge before every big corner (more than 90° within 20 m). Their spacing uses the circuits’ 0.12 plan scale, so they stand 18, 12 and 6 m before the turn-in, which is about where the GT starts braking from top speed. Where the straight is too short, the farthest boards are dropped. The numbers are painted into the trackside texture, so they stay sharp up close and steady at distance, and nothing collides with them.

A chequered start/finish band covers the painted line, with a chequered post on either side of the road. The chequer texture is generated at startup and mipmapped in linear light with 16× anisotropic filtering, so it stays crisp at distance without shimmer. The band adds one draw call; the posts share the boards’ mesh. The car drives through both. As a lap ends the band glows gold for a moment, and a chequered flag on a staff above the left-hand post waves for three seconds (a flag and a staff, drawn only while they wave); a new best brings a short cheer from the crowd with the chime. Reduced motion keeps the band plain and the flag down.

Every start has a countdown. The car waits on the grid, 40.5 m before the line, under three red lights that come on a second apart, then goes on green. The clock still starts at the line, so every lap is still a flying lap and ghosts saved before the countdown remain valid. Throttle held through the lights launches the car on the first physics step after GO; nothing moves before it, so there is no jump start. A new circuit, driving mode or car gets the full 3.8 s countdown; **R** gets a 0.6 s one. **Esc** freezes the countdown and resuming continues it. A short beep marks each light and a higher one GO; **F8** silences them with the tyres. In multiplayer the same lights show the session's own three-second countdown, agreed between both Macs, and both cars go on the same green. The lights are one small UI panel built at startup, changing a few colours a second.

Sectors are coloured the way timing screens colour them. A bar under the clock has one segment per sector, filling in as the lap goes, and each sector notice takes the same colour: **purple** is the fastest that sector has ever been driven on this circuit and mode, **green** beats the same sector of your best lap, **yellow** is slower than it. A sector of a lap that is already invalid never counts. The best-ever sectors are saved beside the ghost (`<circuit>.sectors`, fingerprinted like the lap, so a changed layout starts them afresh) and removed with it by **Reset this ghost**. The bar is eight small UI nodes whose colours change only when a sector closes. `TODORA_SPLITS=PGY` sets the bar for a visual check.

Todora remembers what you chose. `settings.json`, beside the saved laps (`~/Library/Application Support/Todora` on a Mac, `~/.local/share/Todora` on Linux), keeps the circuit, car, setup and driving mode, music and tyre sound, and the ghost toggle, so the game opens where you left it. Every field has a default: a file from an older version fills in what it lacks, an unknown field is ignored, out-of-range numbers are held to their range, and an unreadable file means defaults and one log line. It is written a second after the last change, on a thread of its own, beside the real file and moved into place. Visual checks start from the defaults and never write it.

**Settings** in the pause menu opens a page of four tabs — Audio, Display, HUD and Controls — driven like the pause: up and down choose a row, left and right change it (Enter or A steps it forward, which flips a switch), LB and RB or Q and E change tab, and Esc, B or Start go back to the pause. A click chooses a row and steps it. Changes apply at once and are saved a second later. Radio and tyre sound can still be flipped with **N** and **F8**; whichever side moved is copied to the other. `TODORA_SCREEN=settings TODORA_TAB=1` opens the page for a visual check.

What each row does. **Audio**: radio on or off and its volume, tyre sound and start beeps on or off and their volume. **Display**: full screen (borderless), vertical sync, 4× anti-aliasing, a frame limit of 30, 60, 120 or 144 fps (a sleep at the end of the frame), render scale (50%, 75% or 100%: below 100% the world is drawn into a smaller image that a second camera stretches over the window, so at half scale it costs a quarter of the pixels while the HUD stays sharp at full resolution), text and HUD size (90–150%), a TV-safe margin that moves every HUD panel 5% in from the edges, field of view (35–75°) and a frame-rate readout. **HUD**: km/h or mph, the mini-map, the g-meter, and the countdown (always full, short on restart, or off). **Controls**: steering sensitivity, which bends the stick's response curve without changing full lock, the stick dead zone, and rumble. **Accessibility**, in the HUD and Controls tabs: colour-blind colours (blue for ahead and faster, orange for behind and slower, violet for the fastest ever, and the sector notice says FASTEST, FASTER or SLOWER as well); a high-contrast HUD with opaque black panels; reduced motion, which stops the clock flashing on a new best; tap-to-hold pedals, where a tap latches throttle or brake on and the next tap, the other pedal or a restart lets it go; and Beginner driving assists, which take half the steering from the AI's line and brake where it would. An assisted lap says **ASSISTED** on its card, counts for your own best and medals, and is never uploaded. Text size is the UI-scale row. **Camera** (Display tab, or **V** / **D-pad up** while driving) picks the chase camera as it has always been, a closer and lower chase, or a bonnet view riding rigidly on the car and looking down the road. With **Rumble** on, the pad buzzes low on the grass and lighter on the kerbs, its fine motor rises as the tyres give up grip, and it pulses at GO, on a wall hit and for a new best; everything stops on pause. Continuous rumble is short pulses renewed ten times a second, so nothing is left running. It goes through gilrs, which rumbles pads on Linux and Windows; on macOS it depends on the pad. **Keys** rebinds throttle, brake, both steering directions, handbrake, restart, ghost, camera and leaderboard, each to a key and a pad button: Enter on a row waits five seconds for the key or button, Esc gives up, and a key another action had is swapped rather than lost. The arrow keys, Shift for the brake, the sticks and the analogue triggers always work too, and Escape, Enter and Start stay the menus'. Bindings arrived with version 2 of `settings.json`; a version-1 file gets the defaults. **Language** (HUD tab) is Automatic — the system's, from the locale or macOS's preferred languages — or English, Deutsch, Français, Español or Italiano; everything changes at once: the pause, title, settings, HUD, first-drive and lap cards, the leaderboard, replay, going online, ghost resets, medals, all fifty achievements, the circuit menu and the garage. Circuit names, player names and save files stay as they are. Every string lives in the tables in `src/text.rs`, and a test holds each to having all five languages, the same placeholders and a length that fits. Nothing is re-applied on a frame where no setting changed. `TODORA_SETTINGS='{"units":"mph"}'` starts a visual check from those settings.

Every finished lap puts up a card at the bottom of the screen for five seconds, without taking a key: the lap number and time, then what it meant (**NEW BEST −0.42**, **+0.18 to best**, or **INVALID**), every sector in its colour, and the top speed and slowest point of the lap in the chosen units. An invalid lap says where it was lost instead — all four wheels off in sector 3, fetched back to the road, or paused during shared practice. The card is built once and filled in only when a lap ends; a pause hides it. `TODORA_SUMMARY=best|valid|invalid` holds it up for a visual check.

Beating a best that already stood is celebrated: a **NEW BEST** banner with the time and the gain rises under the start lights for 2.4 seconds, the lap clock flashes gold three times, and three rising notes play through the effects channel (so **F8** silences them). The first lap on a circuit is a best as well, but beating nothing gets the card and not the fanfare.

Every circuit and mode has four medals to chase. The **author** time is a lap driven by hand; **gold** is 2% slower, **silver** 6%, **bronze** 12%. The circuit menu shows your best time and medal on each card, and the circuit brief lists all four targets with the ones you have earned lit, plus what the next asks for (`GOLD — 0.60 to author`). The lap summary card says the same for the lap just driven. Medals are read off your saved best, so nothing new is stored; each medal is a coloured badge with its name written beside it. The author times live in `src/medals/author.rs`, written by `TODORA_AUTHOR=1 cargo test --locked --release --lib write_the_author_times -- --ignored` from the best laps saved on the machine it runs on. Where no lap is saved it uses the plain AI's lap × 0.78, marked provisional: on Red Bull Ring, the one circuit with both, a hand-driven lap took 0.76 of the AI's time. Each entry carries its circuit's fingerprint and a test fails when a layout change leaves one stale. The menu reads saved best times from the lap files' headers and last samples only, once at startup.

A first launch opens on three short cards over the grid — how to drive, what makes a lap, and the ghost — and the countdown waits until they are closed. **A / Enter** moves on and **B / Esc** skips the lot; they name the keys or the pad buttons, whichever was touched last. They never return by themselves: **How to drive** in the pause menu shows them again. If three laps in a row do not count, a line suggests Beginner mode, once ever. Both are remembered in `settings.json`. `TODORA_GUIDE=0 TODORA_DEVICE=pad` shows a card for a visual check.

**Replay and photo**, in the pause menu, plays a lap again with a car of its own while yours waits where you paused: your best, your last lap that counted, or a downloaded ghost's (**Q / E** or **LB / RB**). **A / Enter** plays and pauses, **← →** scrub, **↑ ↓** set the speed from a quarter to four times, and **Y** changes camera between TV-style cameras placed every 70 m along the circuit, the chase camera and the bonnet. **X / P** is photo mode: a free camera within 15 m of the car (WASD or the left stick to move, the arrows or right stick to look, Q / E or the triggers for height) with the HUD gone, and **Enter / A** saves a PNG to `~/Pictures/Todora`. It runs on the real clock, so nothing of the paused lap moves.

The game opens on a title screen over a slow orbit of the circuit you drove last: **Drive**, **Circuits**, **World leaderboard**, **This week's challenge**, **Settings** and **Quit**, chosen like the pause menu. The world waits behind it, so the countdown and the first-drive cards begin with Drive; Settings and the leaderboard come back to the title. A change of circuit fades in from black over a third of a second. Visual checks skip the title.

A lap can be driven again from its inputs alone, to the bit, on any machine — the groundwork for checking online lap times on a server. The engine is given the pedals and wheel as 8-bit values, the same ones a recording keeps (a 256th of pedal travel), and takes its sines, cosines and arctangents from the pure-Rust `libm` rather than from each platform's maths library, which differ in the last bit. `determinism` drives twenty seconds on every circuit, replays them from the recorded inputs, and compares the surface fingerprint and a digest of every step with `src/car/determinism.txt`: macOS arm64, Linux arm64 and Linux x86-64 agree on all forty. The AI's lap times moved by at most 0.03 s. Every lap is also recorded as a **run** — the car's exact state as it crossed the line and the 8-bit pedals and wheel for every physics step until it crossed again — and a valid new best is kept in `outbox/` beside the laps, whether or not you have gone online, until it can be sent. A run is a few kilobytes (`TODORUN1`, little-endian, inputs run-length encoded) and is refused whole if any byte is out of place. `online::replay` drives a run again with the same engine and lap judge and says whether it is the lap it claims to be: the entry must be a car at the line, facing along the road, no faster than the car can go and not already sliding; the lap must finish on the claimed step with the claimed sectors, and count. The lap clock counts whole physics steps and multiplies once, so the time on the board and the time in the run agree to the microsecond rather than drifting by milliseconds over a lap. **World leaderboards** are opt-in. After your first lap that counts, the next time the car waits on the grid the game asks once whether to put your laps on the world boards, under a suggested name you can change; **Settings → Online** changes the answer, the name (type it, or ← → for ideas) and the country, shows laps waiting to upload, and deletes everything the server holds about you (press twice). A background thread signs every request with a key made on this machine (`identity.json`, never sent), sends valid new bests from the outbox as soon as it can, and backs off from 30 seconds to an hour while offline; the game only ever reads a channel. A lap that reaches the board shows its world place in a corner for a few seconds. **L** (or **World leaderboard** in the pause) opens the board for the circuit and mode you are driving: world (top ten, then you ±5), country, and up to five pinned rivals (**X / P**); **← →** browse circuits and **A / Enter** downloads a lap's ghost, which the game drives again with its own engine on a background task (a lap that does not replay is not raced) and runs beside you in cool white, waiting at the line and starting with your clock like your own. **G** then steps through both ghosts, yours, theirs and neither; the gap follows whichever is shown, with a second line (`WR +4.87`, or their name) when both are. With the world record's ghost downloaded, purple means faster than the world in that sector. **This week** is the weekly challenge: one circuit per ISO week, from Monday 00:00 UTC, picked from a fixed shuffle of all forty so every copy of the game agrees without asking anyone and none repeats within forty weeks. Any car, Regular mode. The view shows the circuit, the time left, your best this week (kept in `settings.json`) and, online, that week's board ranked by laps set during it; **F / Y** drives it. A lap that beats your weekly best is uploaded even when it is not your best ever there. The last view, **Records**, lists all forty circuits for the mode you drive with your best, its medal and your world place as last seen, and the medal totals; **A / Enter** on one opens its board. It works offline from your saved laps and the cached boards. **Awards** lists fifty achievements — from finishing a first lap and lapping all forty circuits, through every medal, gold on 5, 20 and 40 circuits, laps in all three modes and cars, streaks of clean laps, purple sectors and 1,000 km, to a world record, beating a downloaded ghost, the weekly challenge, and gold at sixteen of the best-known circuits — with the ones you have earned lit. They are checked only when something happens, announced with a toast, and kept with when they were earned in `achievements.json`. Boards are fetched only once you are online, and the last one seen is shown offline with its age. `TODORA_BOARD_URL` points the game at another server, such as one running locally. The leaderboard server lives in `server/`: one binary and one SQLite file that link the game and drive every submitted lap again before it counts, refuse copies of other people's laps, and keep only a random id, a public key, a name and an optional country per player — [how it works, its API, how to run and deploy it](docs/server.md), and [what it stores and why](docs/privacy.md). `PHYSICS_VERSION` is bumped whenever lap times can move; `TODORA_BLESS=1 cargo test --locked --lib determinism` rewrites the fixture after a deliberate change.

Beside the road stand a covered grandstand and a crowd facing the pits (garages, pit wall and pit lane) across the start line, with team flags on the roof. There are tyre walls with red and white belts round the outside of big corners, marshal posts with yellow flags, advertising boards for made-up brands along the straights, and pines, leafy trees and bushes in clumps out on the grass. Trees keep at least 6.5 m from any road, so they never come between the chase camera and the car. Every piece is placed to stay clear of the roads, bridges, chevron plaques, braking boards and each other, and comes out the same on every visit to a circuit. Together with the braking boards and start posts, they make two unlit meshes of 17,000–31,000 triangles, built in under 20 ms when a circuit loads. Shapes carry shading baked into their colours from the sun. Fine detail (lettering, board numbers, chequers, tyre belts and the crowd) is painted into one texture generated at startup and mipmapped in linear light, so it fades evenly with distance instead of shimmering. Nothing is laid on top of anything else: a detailed face replaces the plain one, so no two surfaces fight over the same depth, and a test checks this on every circuit. That is two draw calls with no per-frame work. They are visual only: the car drives straight through them.

All forty racing directions have been [audited](docs/track-screening/directions.md). Paul Ricard now runs clockwise and Marina Bay anticlockwise; Buenos Aires was already correct. Ghosts from the two reversed layouts are automatically rejected.

Todora 0.10.0 adds a circular mini-map, 4–8 mini-sectors per circuit, driveable surrounding grass with 25% less off-track resistance, persistent lap invalidity when all four wheels leave asphalt and kerbs, and a subtle rubbered racing path. Earlier ghosts are not loaded under the new track-limit rules. [Implementation and local testing notes](docs/testing/free-drive-features.md).

## Design history

The notes and benchmark figures below record earlier proportions and tuning. The current dimensions are above; `cargo test --locked --lib the_circuits -- --ignored --nocapture` reports the current circuits.

The car is the ruler (~2.4 m long, ~1.1 m wide). Almost every circuit is shrunk the same way — the same 0.4⁄3 in plan, the same 0.28 in elevation, the same 3.3 m road — so they are comparable: laps run from Magny-Cours' 497 m to Spa's 1,011 m, and Spa is half again as long as Spielberg in life and comes out half again as long here. Scaling each circuit to a target length instead would have made the long ones tighter and the short ones emptier, and the same lap time would have meant a different thing on each.

Five of them are not shrunk the same way, and that is the one place this promise is broken on purpose. Baku, Monaco, Miami, Jeddah and Suzuka come within a couple of metres of themselves somewhere — round the old town walls, at the harbour, in the stadium car park, on the corniche, at the hairpin — and no road narrow enough to fit there is a road. So each carries a multiplier of its own on top of the shared scale, the smallest that makes room: Baku 2.45, Monaco 1.6, Miami 1.45, Jeddah 1.2, Suzuka 1.1. Seconds, metres, the car and the road stay common between all forty; the ratio of one lap's length to another's does not, and Baku at 1,930 m is now more than twice Spa. The circuit menu shows every lap length for that reason, and a record is never divided by a multiplier to compare it with another — cornering and acceleration do not scale linearly, so a lap of Baku over 2.45 is not a lap of anything.

The alternative was a road that changes width from circuit to circuit, which is worse: the difficult circuit would get a different amount of steering room as well as being difficult, and Baku would need a road narrower than two cars before there was any verge at all. The other alternative was raising the shared scale for all forty, which makes every existing race substantially longer to pay for five.

The hills come from a real elevation model and are kept as steep as it says, within a backstop. That backstop used to do the shaping rather than back it up: at 12% nearly half of Spielberg and well over half of Spa came out pinned to it, so every hill was the same ramp and only its length varied. Eau Rouge had neither a dip to drop into nor a climb to haul out of. At 20% the model shapes it again — Eau Rouge steepens through the compression the way it should, and a long descent now carries the car past its own top speed, because the engine stops pushing at the limiter and gravity does not.

The corners used to be exaggerated, and they had to be. The plan is shrunk about seven and a half times and the road was 8 m wide, which made it five times too wide for the land it was laid on: left alone that turns a chicane into a straight, because the whole of Monza's Rettifilo displaced this car by less than half a road width and the quick way through it was not to steer. Monza was pushed three and a half times away from its own mean line and Spa twice, to buy their corners back.

The road is 3.3 m now and neither of them needs it. Every circuit is as surveyed. That is most of what the narrower road was for: an 8 m road was the thing every other compromise in the game was paying for, and it was paying in corners that had to be amplified, in a 10 m minimum corner radius that ate more than a third of the source's circuits alive, and in a 5.5 m envelope that more than half of them could not clear. At 3.3 m the road is three cars wide rather than seven — half again as generous as a real circuit is, in car widths, instead of two and a half times as generous — and it is no longer the thing in the way.

`Circuit::corners` stays, at 1, because `corners_are_corners` is still the guard: it lays a 40 m line down the circuit at every station and asks whether it stays on the asphalt, and a circuit that came out mostly straight can still be pushed away from its own mean line until it has not. Nothing in the game needs that today. A 40 m straight fits down between 2% of Monaco and 46% of Monza, against a bar of 60%.

The corner target moved with the road, from 10 m to 5 m, and that is the other half of it. Opening a corner cuts it, and at 10 m the cutting was enormous: Monaco came out as a 105 m ring with a quarter of its lap left in it. At 5 m every circuit in the source keeps more than four fifths — Monaco is lowest at 81% — because the outermost rib of a 3.3 m road has room inside a 5 m corner and the outermost rib of an 8 m road does not.

The circuit is one closed spline plus one cross-section fitted to it. Tarmac, edge lines, kerbs and grass are strips of a single loft over the same stations, so they share their edges exactly — nothing overlaps anything except the corner markers below and the underside of Suzuka's bridge, and both of those do it on purpose. The road never changes: 3.3 m across the outsides of the kerbs, the same on every circuit and at every point of one, of which 2.64 m is asphalt, with a 0.08 m white line and then a 0.25 m kerb either side. It is not the old 8 m road multiplied down — a kerb scaled by 0.41 is a strip a wheel cannot land on and a line scaled by 0.41 is too thin to see, so what gives is the asphalt, which had 2.6 m of room either side of the car and now has 0.86. The verge either side narrows to whatever the circuit has room for.

It is fitted *where it is swept*, which is the part that matters. It used to be fitted once for the whole lap, to the tightest corner anywhere on the circuit and the closest the circuit came to itself anywhere on it, so one pinch cost every metre of road its shoulder and a circuit with one pinch too many was turned away outright. Now the fit is asked at every station and on each side separately, because a circuit that runs back past itself is cramped on the side it runs back on and roomy on the other, and a corner only threatens the verge on the inside of it. Thirty-one of the thirty-nine carry the full 3.65 m either side for the whole of the lap; the other eight draw in somewhere and are back at 3.65 by the time the corner is over, the narrowest of them being Zandvoort at 2.41 m.

What decides it is one measurement, taken at every station on each side: grow a circle out from the centreline, tangent to the road, until it touches any other part of the circuit. Everything inside that circle belongs to this station and to nothing else, so a rib anywhere inside it cannot have come from somewhere else as well. That single number is both of the bounds the old fit needed and neither of their weaknesses — where the circuit is turning it runs out of room at the centre of *this* corner, and where the circuit doubles back it runs out half way across *this* gap. It also needs no arbitrary exclusion of nearby stations to work, because the circle is tangent to the road, so the road running on ahead is tangent to it too and never inside it. The old measure had to ignore every pair of stations within 60 m of each other along the lap, which is also how far apart two genuinely separate turns can be.

From that, two bounds with two different margins. On the inside of a bend the outermost rib leaves a seventh of the radius unused, which is a fraction because what it protects scales with the corner. Against another stretch of circuit it leaves a quarter of a metre, which is a distance because what it protects does not — two roads passing need to not touch, and 0.25 m of grass between them is 0.25 m whether the gap was 5 m or 15. Paul Ricard's two verges used to meet *exactly*, because the old fit took the whole of half the gap and stopped. Nothing folded; nothing was clear either. Then the widths are tapered into their neighbours, never widening, so the edge of the mesh and the wall under the car do not jump from station to station.

A verge is described by its slopes rather than by the heights they arrive at, which is what lets it be any width: grass falling at 12.5% for the first two thirds, then a lip at 75%. On two metres of verge that comes to −0.12 m and −0.62 m. On a narrow shoulder it comes to less, rather than the same drop being crammed into a fifth of the run — which would have been a cliff with a corner marker leaning off it. The verge is sized against the car rather than against the road, because what it is for is somewhere to put a car that has run out of road, and a spun one is 2.4 m long.

A shoulder still has to be a shoulder, and what that means is now a sum of what has to fit on one rather than a round number. It used to be 1.5 m of verge, which is a pleasant amount of grass and was not measuring anything — a stretch of circuit is not unbuildable for having 1.4. It is now 0.75 m, which is the wall the car is held at, and the footprint of a corner marker with its clearances on grass that is two thirds of the verge, whichever is larger. Lowering the bar is not the same as removing it: `a_circuit_with_nowhere_to_put_a_shoulder_is_still_refused` builds an oval whose two straights run 9 m apart and watches it be turned away with the place named.

And "nothing overlaps anything" is now something asked rather than argued. Every rib of every cross-section, at the stations and at the seams between them, is handed back to the circuit to locate: it has to come back as the station it was swept from, at the offset it was swept at. If any part of the surface had grown over any other part, the lookup would find the other one.

Asking where the car is, is the question the game asks most: every wheel, every tyre mark, the camera, the clock and the ghost, several times per fixed step. It used to be answered by projecting the car onto every segment of the lap, of which there are between 1,244 and 4,824. Now the ribbon carries an index of itself — a tree of plan bounding boxes over runs of consecutive segments, descending into the nearer box first and leaving unopened any box already further away than the best point found. A lookup reads between 12 and 36 segments however long the lap is, which is thirteen times quicker on the same question, and the test suite that builds every circuit several times over went from 40 seconds to 3.

It is the same answer, not a close one. `the_index_agrees_with_the_walk` holds the whole fix — the point, the heading, the lateral offset, the distance round the lap — to exact equality against the old exhaustive scan, on every circuit, at every station, seam, road edge, far-off probe and point in the sky. Where two segments are exactly as near, the earlier one wins, as it did before, and a square asked about its own centre is the fixture that ties eight ways and says so.

Corner indicators are flat charcoal plaques with a muted chevron pointing into the bend. They sit only on the outside verge, retaining the previous marker positions and 8.8 m spacing within each marked stretch. Only the nearest three within 60 m along the road are shown; the fourth and the inside markers are omitted.

Approach markers use the direction of the upcoming bend to choose the outside verge. Selection follows the circuit through the lap seam and crossings, including when driving backwards. Each plaque follows the ground, sits 1.5 cm above it and has no collision or cast shadow. Tests cover placement, clearance, geometry and the three-marker limit across all circuits.

**W** and **S** are the pedals — **S** brakes while you are rolling and reverses once you have stopped. **A** and **D** steer, **Space** is the handbrake, **R** starts again — car on the grid, clock at zero, marks wiped — **T** opens the list of circuits and **C** the garage, **Scroll** zooms. Arrow keys work too, and **Shift** still brakes. In every menu — the title, pause, circuits, garage, settings, leaderboard and replay — **W A S D** move as the arrows do. In the circuit list they browse until you type another letter, which starts a search; **/** first starts a search that begins with one of them. On the name field in Settings they are letters. **1**, **2** and **3** slide the setup between understeer, balanced and oversteer. **G** shows and hides the ghost. **Esc** stops the game where it stands and **Enter** starts it again.

On an Xbox controller, the left stick steers, **A** accelerates, **X** brakes/reverses and **B** applies the handbrake. RT/LT also work as analogue pedals. **RB** resets the car to the start, **LB** opens the garage, **View** opens circuits, **Y** toggles the ghost and **Start** pauses/resumes. The D-pad's down brakes/reverses and left/right steer, like the arrow keys; **D-pad up** changes the camera. Stick up/down is reserved for menus; change handling in the garage or with 1/2/3. In menus, **LB / RB** switch between Circuits and Garage & setup, the stick or D-pad browses, **A** confirms and **B** or **Start** goes back.

Choose a driving mode in **Garage & setup** by clicking its button or cycling with **M** / controller **Y**, then **Apply & drive**:

| Mode | Top speed |
|---|---|
| Beginner | 80% of Regular |
| Regular (default) | Current driving speeds |
| Pro | 120% of Regular |

Modes change actual driving speed for all three cars; the speedometer reflects it. The percentages describe flat-road top speed, with hills and corners still affecting pace. Handling setup remains a separate choice. Applying a mode restarts the lap, and each mode keeps its own best times and ghosts. Existing saved laps remain under Regular. Resetting the car or changing circuits keeps the selected mode.


Every lap is a flying lap. The car is set down 40.5 m short of the start/finish line (45 m before the laps were shortened; the figures below are from then) and the clock is armed by the line rather than by the car moving off, so the run up to it is yours to spend and costs nothing. 45 m is what there is to give: the car reaches 20 m/s in it, of the 22.2 m/s it actually settles at — `top_speed` is 24, but that is only where the engine's push fades out, and drag and rolling resistance are still there when it does — and the last tenth of that would cost another 65 m. The run-up also wants to be straight, or the car arrives slower for having cornered on the way, and straight is what a few circuits have none of behind their lines. Most cross their own line between 19 and 20 m/s; the slowest is Silverstone at 14.2, because the Hamilton Straight is short and 45 m back from its line is Club. `the_run_up_reaches_the_line_at_speed` holds the lot of them above 8.

A menu is a pause with something to read. **T** lists the circuits and **C** the cars; the arrow keys move, **Enter** takes the row under the cursor and **Esc** backs out having changed nothing. Forty circuits do not fit on a screen, so the circuit list is a window thirteen rows tall onto itself — one that only moves when the cursor would otherwise walk off it, keeping three rows of road ahead, because a list that recentred on every step is a list where nothing stays where you last saw it. Each row carries the length of a lap of it, which the list did not need to say while every circuit was shrunk alike and does now. Both open with the cursor on what is already being driven, so opening one and pressing **Enter** does nothing at all. They stop the world through the very same mechanism the pause key uses, so there is no second idea of "not driving now" to keep in agreement with the first — the car is already still, the clock is already held, and the driver's keys are already going nowhere. The two modules own opposite ends of that one piece of state and never reach into each other's: the pause takes **Esc** only from a running game and **Enter** only from a paused one, so while a menu is up both keys belong to the menu.

A pause is the clock, not a second game running slowly behind it. Everything Todora does is driven by one virtual clock: the engine steps `Time<Fixed>`, which that clock fills, and the lap timer counts those same fixed steps. Stop the clock and both stop, together and exactly, because neither is given anything — no system has to be told it is paused and none of them can drift out of step with another. What a pause cannot stop is the driver's hands, so the controls are let go of on the way in and the keys are not read again until the way out: while the game is stopped, nothing the driver presses reaches the car, the reset key and the setup slider included.

A restart is for the lap, not for the day. It gives up the lap in progress and nothing else: the laps you have driven, the best of them and the ghost all stay, because you press **R** when the lap has gone wrong and what you are chasing is the lap you are not driving. Changing circuit or driving mode loads the records for that circuit and mode.

The handling is an arcade model, not a tyre simulation: a velocity, a heading, and a grip figure that says how far the two may disagree. Steering sets how fast the car rotates; grip is how much sideways speed the tyres can scrub off each frame. Ask a corner for more than that and the car keeps rotating while its velocity does not follow — the difference is the slide, and the tyre marks. Let go and the slide pulls the nose back into line. The handbrake and a heavy right foot at the limit both pull grip down, which is where the drifts come from; braking never does, so the brakes always work however hard you are turning. A slide costs speed — scrubbing rubber turns it into heat, and a rear that has let go spins its wheels rather than driving — so drifting is a technique, not a free lunch: hold the throttle through one and it bleeds, lift and it catches. Grip rises with speed, so a fast corner is a wide one rather than a lost one, and braking into a corner tightens it rather than loosening it — arriving too fast, the intuitive thing to do is the right one. Off the throttle the engine holds the car back hard — about half of what the brakes do — so the throttle is the speed control and the brakes are for the big stops: lift for a corner and the car settles into it. It also means a hill only gives speed while you are on the power. The grass is a gravel trap: at pace it costs most of a g and the wheels spin, so going off ends the corner, though a car can always crawl back to the road. The meter top left shows speed, what the seat feels, and where the setup slider sits.

Your fastest lap drives again alongside you as a ghost — a translucent copy of the car replaying it in step with the current lap's clock — and the panel top right shows the gap to it: green and negative when this lap is ahead at this point of the circuit, red and positive when behind. The gap is keyed to where you are, not to the clock, which is what makes it something you can drive against corner by corner.

The ghost outlives the session. Beat your own time and the lap is written out — a few thousand poses against the clock, one file per circuit, under `Library/Application Support/Todora/laps` on a Mac. Come back tomorrow and it is on the grid with you, its time already on the board as **BEST**. The file carries a fingerprint of the finished driving surface — not of the trace it came from, so it moves when anything that shapes a circuit moves, the scales and the smoothing and the grade backstop included. The centreline used to be the whole of it, which was a complete description for exactly as long as every circuit carried the same verge from its first station to its last; now that the verge is fitted station by station, a circuit can keep its centreline to the bit and put the wall a metre further in, so the cross-section goes into the hash as well. Paint does not, on purpose: recolouring a kerb stripe changes the mesh, changes nothing a lap time depends on, and throwing away everyone's ghosts over it would only teach people to distrust the check. A lap saved around one shape is quietly not a lap any more rather than a car driving through the scenery. Nothing about saving is allowed to be a problem: nowhere to write, a half-written file, a file somebody has edited — each one means no ghost, said once in the log, and a game that carries on.

Three cars, and the same shooting brake three times over — the promise that everything a car is lives in one `Handling` value, cashed: a second car is data rather than code. **C** opens the garage. The **Tourer** is the one the game has always shipped, green, and the middle of everything. The **Clubman** is red and holds on: more grip and more downforce, and it runs out of engine at 20.1 m/s where the Tourer goes to 22.2. The **Express** is silver and does the opposite — least grip, and still pulling at 27.6 m/s. They are balanced by lapping rather than by taste: the plain driver takes all three round every circuit, and each of them is quickest somewhere.

They are closer together than they were, and the narrower road is why. A 3.3 m road with 5 m corners is a road made mostly of corners, and grip is worth far more than top speed on one: at the old spread of a fifth either way the grippy car was quickest on all thirty-nine, by as much as 12%. So the grip spread came in to about a fifteenth and the speed spread went out — 12.8, 14.0 and 14.8 of grip against 31.0, 24.0 and 21.5 of top speed. `top_speed` does two jobs in the engine, because it is also where the engine's push fades out, so a car with more of it pulls harder out of a corner as well as going faster down a straight; that second job is what pays the low-grip car back on a circuit with no straights to speak of.

| | settles at | hardest stop | Imola | Suzuka | Las Vegas |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tourer | 22.2 m/s | 12.6 m | **52.99** | 69.61 | 62.25 |
| Clubman | 20.1 m/s | 9.8 m | 53.05 | **69.10** | 62.57 |
| Express | 27.6 m/s | 20.7 m | 54.14 | 71.45 | **61.72** |

Over all forty the Tourer is quickest on eleven, the Clubman on twenty-two and the Express on eight, and the widest any circuit spreads them is 4.6% — Albert Park. Silverstone and Watkins Glen spread them by 0.2%. `no_car_is_the_car_to_pick` holds both halves of that: nobody far off the pace anywhere, so the menu has no wrong answer in it, and the quickest car not the same one everywhere, so it has no right one. A menu with either is not a choice. The stars in it are held to the numbers too — a car cannot be retuned into disagreeing with its own entry. Changing car gives up the lap in progress, because half a lap in one car and half in another is not a lap in either; the board and the ghost stay, because they belong to the circuit.

The setup slider and the garage meet in one order: the car is the baseline and the slider is the lean on top of it. Each notch scales what the car brings rather than replacing it, so the middle notch is exactly the car as it is and the grippy car on oversteer is a grippy car that has been loosened — not a different car wearing its badge. Setting the five dials outright, which is what the slider used to do, would have made all three cars identical at both ends of the slider and different only in the middle.

There is no front and rear axle here to hand grip to, so understeer and oversteer are built out of what the model does have — the slide, the angle between where the car points and where it is going. The slider moves five dials together: how much more turn full lock asks for than the grip can give, how hard the nose is pulled back into line, how much grip the throttle spends at the limit, how hard a rear the driver has let go throws the tail round, and how eagerly the car rotates. That fourth one is what makes oversteer oversteer — on the loose setup, full throttle in a corner brings the tail round and the car turns more than the wheel asked; lift, and it catches. Every notch still turns at full lock and still refuses to spin; which one is quickest depends on who is driving. A driver who is still learning the circuit covers more ground on understeer, and one who is not is fractionally quicker on oversteer. The engine steps at a fixed 240 Hz whatever the frame rate, so a slow machine and a fast one drive the same car, and everything a car is lives in one `Handling` value, which is what makes the garage three numbers rather than three code paths.

Needs a recent stable Rust (`rustup`, or `omarchy install dev-env rust` on Omarchy). First Bevy compile is slow; later ones are not.

## Layout

| Path | What belongs there |
|---|---|
| `src/main.rs` | Binary entry. Calls `todora::run()`. |
| `src/lib.rs` | `GamePlugin` — register new feature plugins here. |
| `src/<feature>.rs` | One plugin per feature (`input`, `camera`, `world`, `lap`, `hud`, `skid`, `pause`, `menu`). |
| `src/car/` | `physics.rs` is the engine: a pure `step` over a `Car`, its `Handling` (everything that makes one car drive like itself, as a value) and a `Surface`. `garage.rs` is the three cars, each a `Handling` and a colour. `setup.rs` is the slider that leans whichever of them is being driven. `mod.rs` is the entity — the leaning body, the wheels, the paint, and `advance`, which carries `Controls` through the engine in fixed substeps. `driver.rs` is the AI: it laps the circuit in the tests today and drives opponents tomorrow. |
| `src/track/` | `circuits/` is one file per circuit, each a real trace in real metres plus a line about where it passes over itself, and `circuits/mod.rs` is the list the circuit menu is drawn from; `ribbon.rs` turns a trace into a centreline, opening the corners too tight to loft and exaggerating the ones too shallow to drive, indexes it so that finding the nearest point does not mean reading the whole lap, and measures how much room it has at any point of itself; `markers.rs` places three flat chevron indicators ahead on the outside verge; `profile.rs` is the cross-section — the mesh, the height under a wheel and the grip under it all read from one shape — and fits one to each station of a circuit; `mod.rs` is the `Track` the game asks where the ground is, and the switch that builds whichever one the menu asked for. |
| `src/ghost/` | `mod.rs` is the replay and the gap to it; `store.rs` is the lap on disk — where it lives, and what a file has to be before it is believed. |
| `assets/` | Runtime files Bevy loads. glTF lives in `assets/models/`. |
| `art/` | Source art. Blender files in `art/models/`. |
| `tools/` | Generators and one-off scripts. `make_track.py` turns a real circuit into a `src/track/circuits/` module, from a pinned revision of the source. `screen_tracks.py` puts the same geometry questions to every circuit in that source at once. |

New gameplay goes in its own `src` module with a plugin, then gets added to `GamePlugin`. New runtime files go in `assets/`. Source meshes stay in `art/`.

Input runs in `PreUpdate`, after Bevy reads the devices and after the pause has decided whether the driver is being listened to at all; the menus run next, then the circuit they asked for is built, and everything that puts itself back on a reset runs after that — so the circuit is already the new one by the time the car, the clock, the ghost and the marks act on it. Driving runs in `FixedUpdate` at 240 Hz; each step includes track confinement and recovery. The lap clock follows the car on that same clock, then the ghost finishes and records the step. Camera, body animation, ghost replay and HUD run in `Update`, before Bevy propagates transforms for rendering. Keep that order when adding systems. Bevy carries fractional steps between frames and caps a stall at its default 250 ms of virtual time; driving and lap timing share that cap.

## Another circuit

```sh
tools/make_track.py be-1925 spa_francorchamps "Spa-Francorchamps"
cargo test --locked --lib the_menu_shows_the_lap
```

Nearly the whole job. The id is a circuit in [bacinger/f1-circuits](https://github.com/bacinger/f1-circuits), which traces centrelines as GeoJSON; the tool projects the trace flat about its own centroid, samples the [Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com/) DEM for heights, writes `src/track/circuits/<name>.rs` and adds it to the list. The trace revision is pinned, so that `master` moving is not the same as a circuit being resurveyed, and the DEM samples are cached under `~/.cache/todora` so that generating forty circuits in a row asks the elevation service each question once. Both are baked into the module the moment it is written, so nothing about the game or its tests reaches for a network. Nothing is scaled there either — `src/track/mod.rs` does that, the same way for every circuit but four.

The second line is the one number the generator cannot know, because it is what comes out the far end: the finished lap length, which the circuit menu shows. The test says what to write down.

The other thing to settle is where the start/finish line goes, because the game reads it off the trace's first sample and off nothing else — the paint, the grid, the lap gate and the distance round the lap all hang from it. The source does not promise to start there. Thirty-seven of the forty did; Monaco began at Massenet, Silverstone on the pre-2011 pit straight between Woodcote and Copse, and Yas Marina a third of a lap out, so those three drove a lap that started in the wrong place. The line is now a coordinate `make_track.py` is told with `--line lat,lon`, kept beside the trace in `docs/track-screening/sources.json` and reapplied whenever a circuit is regenerated. Finding it means finding the pit lane: OpenStreetMap maps one for nearly every circuit here, a dozen metres off the road, and the line belongs on the stretch it runs alongside.

A circuit then has to earn its place, and the tests are what decide. Corners tighter than the loft can carry are opened out before the road is swept, which cuts them; every circuit in the game keeps more than four fifths of its lap that way; a circuit that keeps far less has been rounded off into a ring rather than shrunk, and `every_circuit_survives_the_shrink` is what says so. What a circuit can carry either side of the road it settles for itself, station by station: the 3.3 m road never moves, and the verge narrows wherever the circuit is tight and nowhere else. So adding one is additive twice over — a circuit with less room gets a narrower verge instead of everyone else getting one, and it gets it only where the room is short.

All forty circuits in the source clear it.

It used to be seventeen, and four things moved: a 3.3 m road instead of 8 m, a 5 m corner target instead of 10 m, a cross-section fitted station by station instead of once for the lap, and a bridge. What each of them was worth is in [docs/track-screening](docs/track-screening), which `tools/screen_tracks.py` writes by putting the same questions to all forty at once against a pinned revision of the source. At the old 10 m corner target the narrower road alone gets 25 of them in. At 5 m it gets 35. Four more are the plan-scale exceptions. The last one is Suzuka.

**Monaco** used to be the standing example of a circuit that could not be shrunk: opened to 10 m corners it kept a quarter of its lap, and 3.3 km of street circuit came out as a 105 m ring with nothing in it. At 5 m it keeps 81% and laps 650 m, with 15.6 m of climb up to Casino in it and only 2% of the lap that a 40 m straight line fits down. What it needed on top was a plan multiplier of 1.6 — 1.45 for the harbour, where it passes within 3.5 m of itself, and the rest for the hill, because a plan compressed harder than the height is a plan with steeper hills on it and at 1.45 a quarter of the lap came out pinned to the grade cap.

**Suzuka** crosses over itself, and it is the only circuit in the source that does — the Nürburgring was listed here too and should not have been. Proper segment-intersection testing finds no crossing in `de-1927`, raw or finished. Suzuka's is real in the raw trace and survives splining and corner opening: its back straight goes over the run from the Degner curves to the hairpin, 4,700 m round its lap against 2,326 m, and the plan fit comes out wanting a cross-section of *less than nothing* there because the two stretches pass within 0.12 m of each other.

A circuit is still one closed ribbon carrying one road, and that has not changed — a bridge is not a second ribbon. It is a stretch of the one road that is higher than another stretch of it, with three things taught to notice.

The **fit** stops treating a road overhead as a road in the way, but only within twenty metres of a crossing that the circuit declared. That scoping is the whole of it: Miami has two stretches nearly two metres apart in height and a few metres apart in plan and nothing about that is a bridge, so a rib of the lower one has to keep belonging to the lower one.

The **lookup** answers with a deck rather than a point. Where two roads are at one place on the map and more than a metre apart in height, it picks between them by continuity — the car carries how far round the lap it was last found, and it cannot have got from one deck to the other without driving most of a lap — and where there is no history, by height, which is what a ghost dropped onto a saved pose relies on. The car's answer is what the clock, the wall, the tyre marks and the engine's idea of the slope all read, so all of them are on the deck the driver is on.

The **heights** are solved after the smoothing and the grade cap, because a bridge built before the cap is a bridge the cap takes straight back off. What is solved for is the separation the clearance needs, and what is added is the shortfall: Suzuka's elevation model already reads the back straight six metres above the road it crosses, so nothing is raised there at all, while the flat synthetic figure of eight the feature was built against needs all of it. Where a lift is needed the ground under the bridge is levelled first, so the grade of the finished thing is knowable, and the ramps are raised cosines ten times as long as the rise, which peaks at 15.7% however big the bridge is.

The clearance is the one number that is authored rather than measured, and the data says so. A 90 m ground model reads both stretches at the height of the ground between them, because ground is what it measures; it has nothing to say about a road deck. So the source gives the topology — that there is a crossing, where it is, which stretch is on top — and the game gives 1.6 m of air under the span, which is a metre and a half above a car that stands 1.2 m tall. `a_bridge_says_where_it_came_from` is what stops that line being dropped from the data.

And a bridge has an underside. The loft is one surface seen from above everywhere else; under a span it grows a soffit and a fascia down each side, as deep as a deck and tapering out with the ramps, so that a driver on the lower road passes under something rather than through a one-sided road. `loft_faces_up` does not cover those three strips and is not weakened to: they have a check of their own that asks the same thing of them, which is that every face points out of the solid it is a face of.

All of that is said by tests written for the three circuits that came first, plus four more written for the bridge. That is the point of them.

What every circuit that did get in came out as:

```sh
cargo test --locked --lib the_circuits -- --ignored --nocapture
```

Laps run from Magny-Cours' 497 m to Baku's 1,930 m. Thirty-one of the forty carry the full 3.65 m cross-section for the whole of the lap; the other nine draw in somewhere and are back at 3.65 m by the time the corner is over, the narrowest of them being Zandvoort at 2.41 m. Every one of them keeps at least four fifths of its lap through the corner-opening pass. A 40 m straight fits down between 2% of Monaco and 46% of Monza, against a bar of 60% — and the 40 m is 40 m of the *source* circuit, so the five built at a multiplier are asked the same question about their own layout as the other thirty-five.

## Checks

```sh
cargo fmt --all -- --check
cargo clippy --locked --all-targets -- -D warnings
cargo test --locked --all-targets
```

Tests cover acceleration, braking, reverse, slopes, grass, drift recovery, all three setups on all three cars, wall impacts, lap validation and ghost recording. Every circuit in the game is held to the same bar — it survives the shrink, is still where it was traced and still turns the way its trace turns, carries a road and a shoulder everywhere it goes, has every rib of that road belong to the station it was swept from, tapers its verge rather than stepping it, draws no degenerate triangle, laps as one lap, closes its kerb stripes at the line, starts on the tarmac and reaches its own line at speed, marks its corners with three flat chevron plaques ahead on the outside verge, with the established spacing and clearance from the kerbs and verge edges, keeps most of the relief its elevation model gave it without leaning on the grade cap to shape its hills, has corners a straight line does not fit down, offers the menu the lap it actually builds, and is lapped by both AI drivers within a time budget set by its own length — so a new one either clears it or fails loudly. A circuit that passes over itself is held to four more: the air under its span is the air it was built with, measured from every rib of the deck to whatever is beneath it; its deck is as thick and as long as its own data says; what holds it up faces away from the road; and the plain driver takes it round both ways without ever changing deck, with the lap turning over exactly once. There is a synthetic figure of eight in the tests for the last of those to be asked of something whose crossing is at a known place and a known angle, and which can be rolled round so that its bridge sits on top of the start line. Being *where it was traced* is two measurements retention cannot make: the trace ends up 5.4 m from the finished circuit at worst on average, against a bar of two of the tightest corners the game allows; and a circuit turns through two to five whole turns of corners in a lap where a ring would turn through one, keeping at least three fifths of the turning its trace had. `the_identity_checks_notice_a_circuit_that_has_been_changed` puts both to a circuit that has been moved and to one with its chicanes taken out. The relief is checked as a fraction rather than in metres, because Spa rises 27 m and Monza 6, and Monza is not broken. A restart keeps the board and the ghost while a switch clears both, the track key builds the next circuit before anything puts itself back, and the saved lap is checked both ways round and against every way a file can be wrong. The schedule tests compare equal driving time across different frame rates and stalls, and check that resets and setup changes reach the next physics step. Mixed-input stress tests check finite values and bounded speeds and forces. AI drivers exercise every circuit through the same movement path as the game, which is the net under the hills: a climb the car cannot take or a descent it cannot stop on fails there rather than under the player.

Run the optional handling reports — the garage's balance table among them — with:

```sh
cargo test --locked --lib -- --ignored --nocapture
```

Automated checks do not replace a drive with a keyboard and gamepad when judging steering feel, camera motion and analogue controls.

## A Mac app

tools/package_macos.sh

Builds a release binary, wraps it in `dist/Todora.app` with its icon, and puts that in `dist/Todora.dmg` next to an Applications shortcut. The app is signed ad hoc, which is all this Mac needs; another Mac will ask for right-click → Open the first time, because there is no Developer ID behind it.

## A Linux app

Linux remains a supported single-player platform. Game Center is optional and
macOS-only; enabling its Cargo feature on Linux leaves multiplayer disabled.
Windows is optional and currently untested.

```sh
tools/package_linux.sh --install
```

Builds a release binary into `dist/Todora/` with its assets and icon, then installs it for this user: the game under `~/.local/share/todora`, an icon, and a desktop entry so Omarchy's launcher can find it. Same lockfile as the Mac package. Needs `rsvg-convert` for the icon (`librsvg` on Arch).

Without `--install` it only writes `dist/Todora/`. Run that folder's `todora` binary, or `cargo run --release`.

See [Linux dependencies and verification](docs/testing/linux.md). The Linux
workflow checks builds, tests, rendering and installation on each push and PR.

Rebuild the Omarchy GT from Blender:

```sh
/Applications/Blender.app/Contents/MacOS/Blender --background --python tools/make_omarchy_gt.py
```
